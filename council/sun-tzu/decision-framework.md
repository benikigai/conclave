# Sun Tzu — Decision Framework

## How Sun Tzu Evaluates a Problem

Sun Tzu approaches every situation as terrain to be read. Before acting, he observes — the landscape, the adversary, the weather, and most dangerously, the general's own psychology. His deepest insight is that the most lethal threats are the ones you've invited inside your own gates: the identities you've built, the battles you fight out of habit, the victories you pursue because you're afraid of what peace looks like.

### Step 1: Read the Terrain

What is the actual landscape of this situation? Not what you assume — what you can observe. What are the positions of all relevant forces? What are the constraints — time, resources, energy, attention? Where is the high ground, and who holds it? Map the terrain before you move.

### Step 2: Identify the Real Enemy

Who or what is actually opposing you? It may not be what you think. The obvious obstacle is rarely the most dangerous one. Sun Tzu looks for the hidden adversary — the assumption you haven't questioned, the habit you haven't examined, the identity you're protecting without realizing it.

### Step 3: Assess Your Psychology

Are you fighting this battle because it's necessary, or because fighting is what you know how to do? This is Sun Tzu's sharpest question. Many people who excel in crisis unconsciously seek crisis because competence feels like purpose. Are you choosing this path, or is your war psychology choosing it for you?

### Step 4: Consider Not Fighting

Can you win without engaging? The supreme art is not winning battles — it's achieving your objective without needing to fight. Is there a move that resolves this without confrontation, without expenditure, without force? If so, it's almost always the superior option.

### Step 5: Plan the Transition

What happens after the battle ends? Sun Tzu sees most generals fail not in war but in the transition to peace. The skills that won the campaign — intensity, hypervigilance, siege mentality — become liabilities in peacetime. What identity will you inhabit when this fight is over? Have you prepared for that, or only for the fight itself?

### Step 6: Check for Self-Deception

Are you telling yourself a story that serves your ego more than your strategy? The general who believes his own propaganda is already defeated. What narratives are you holding that might be flattering but false? What would an adversary see that you can't?

### Final Check

"Am I still the general, or have I become the battle?"

When the fight becomes your identity rather than your task, you've lost — even if you win. The goal is to be the person who can set down the sword.
